## Introduction

Almost every chord in The Quietest Hour either belongs to D major or is a dominant chord pointing at one that does. That single idea explains why its 55 bars feel smooth, and why some chords would sound wrong in them. This book teaches the theory behind it, using the bar-by-bar chord sheet as the running example.

**The piece at a glance**

- Key: D major, with its relative minor B minor as a second home.
- Metre and feel: 4/4, swung 16ths, ♩ = 70 rubato, slowing to about ♩ = 40 at the end.
- Form: 55 bars in five sections: Opening (bars 1–8), Main flow (9–22), Emotional peak (23–36), Pensive pullback (37–48), Final descent (49–55).
- Chords: twelve, all with 7ths and most with 9ths, 11ths or 13ths: Gmaj9, F#m7, F#7#5, Bm9, B7#9, E13, Em9, Em11, A13, A7b9, C#m7b5, Dmaj9.

**How to use this book**

1. Read Parts 1–4 in order the first time. They build the vocabulary every later part uses.
2. Keep a guitar in your hands. Every idea is tied to bar numbers; click any bar number to open it in the Bar explorer.
3. Use the tables as reference: keys and relative minors (Part 1), chord building (Part 2), the bar-by-bar Roman numeral map (Appendix).
4. Finish with the practice plan at the end.

**Conventions**

- Sharps and flats are written # and b, as on the chord sheet.
- Roman numerals: upper case for major and dominant chords (IV, V), lower case for minor (ii, vi), ø for half-diminished (iiø).
- "V/vi" reads "five of six": the dominant chord of the vi chord.
- Fret shapes run low E to high e; x means a muted string.

## Part 1 — Foundations: notes, intervals, scales and keys

Western music uses 12 notes, and every chord, scale and key is a pattern of distances between them. Learn the distances (intervals) and everything else becomes counting.

### The 12 notes

C, C#/Db, D, D#/Eb, E, F, F#/Gb, G, G#/Ab, A, A#/Bb, B, then C again an octave higher. Each step is a **half step** (one fret on guitar). Two half steps make a **whole step**. Notes with two names (F# and Gb) are **enharmonic**: same sound, different spelling. The spelling depends on the key and the chord, which is why the chord sheet writes A# in F#7#5 but Bb in A7b9.

### The major scale and the key of D

The major scale follows the step pattern **W W H W W W H** (W = whole step, H = half step). From D that gives **D E F# G A B C#**, so D major has two sharps, F# and C#. Each note of the scale is a **degree**, numbered 1–7, and degrees name the chord tones (3rd, 5th, 7th) and extensions (9th, 11th, 13th) used throughout this book.

### Minor scales and the relative minor

The natural minor scale follows **W H W W H W W**. Start the D major scale on its 6th degree, B, and you get B natural minor: **B C# D E F# G A**. Same seven notes, different home. B minor is the **relative minor** of D major. That shared note set is why Bm9 feels like a second home in The Quietest Hour, and why the piece can lean towards B minor (bars 2–3, 10–11, 46–48) without ever changing key.

Two variants of minor matter for this piece:

- **Harmonic minor** raises the 7th degree (B C# D E F# G **A#**). The A# is the leading tone that pulls up into B, and it is exactly the 3rd of F#7#5.
- **Melodic minor** raises the 6th and 7th going up (B C# D E F# **G# A#**). It is the parent of the altered scale used over F#7#5 and B7#9 (Part 10).

To find a relative minor, go down three half steps from the major key's tonic (D → C# → C → B). To find a relative major, go up three half steps from the minor tonic.

### The circle of fifths

Arrange the keys so each is a perfect 5th above the last: C G D A E B F# C# going one way (adding a sharp each time), and C F Bb Eb Ab Db Gb Cb the other (adding a flat). Keys next to each other share six of seven notes, so they feel closely related. D major's neighbours are G major (one less sharp) and A major (one more). Their relative minors, E minor and F# minor, appear in the piece as Em9/Em11 and F#m7.

The circle also predicts chord movement. A chord whose root falls a 5th (or rises a 4th) to the next root is the strongest move in tonal music: B → E → A → D runs straight round the circle, and it is the backbone of The Quietest Hour (Part 4).

## Part 2 — How chords work

A chord is a stack of 3rds built on a root. The size of each 3rd (major = 4 half steps, minor = 3) decides the chord's quality. Keep stacking and you get 7ths, then 9ths, 11ths and 13ths. After the 13th the stack has used all seven notes of the scale, so it starts repeating.

### Triads: three notes

- **Major** = major 3rd + minor 3rd (D F# A). Bright, stable.
- **Minor** = minor 3rd + major 3rd (B D F#). Darker, still stable.
- **Diminished** = minor 3rd + minor 3rd (C# E G). Tense; the 5th is flat.
- **Augmented** = major 3rd + major 3rd (F# A# D). Unsettled; the 5th is sharp. It sits inside F#7#5.
- **Suspended** chords replace the 3rd with the 2nd (sus2) or 4th (sus4). They sound open because they have no major or minor 3rd.

### Seventh chords: four notes

Add another 3rd on top and you get the chords jazz, soul and neo-soul are built from:

- **maj7** (1 3 5 7): major triad + major 7th. Dreamy, at rest. Gmaj9 and Dmaj9 grow from it.
- **Dominant 7** (1 3 5 b7): major triad + minor 7th. Contains a tritone between 3rd and b7 that wants to resolve. A13, E13, F#7#5, B7#9 and A7b9 are all dominants.
- **m7** (1 b3 5 b7): soft, warm. F#m7, Bm9 and Em9 grow from it.
- **m7b5**, half-diminished (1 b3 b5 b7): C#m7b5. Gently tense, usually leads into a dominant.
- **dim7** (1 b3 b5 bb7): fully symmetrical, very tense.
- **m(maj7)** (1 b3 5 7): minor with a major 7th, film-noir sound.

### Extensions: 9, 11, 13

Extensions are scale notes an octave above the root: **9 = the 2nd**, **11 = the 4th**, **13 = the 6th**. A chord name with 9, 11 or 13 always implies the 7th. Without a 7th you write **add9** or **6** instead.

Extensions add colour without changing function. Gmaj9 still works as a IV chord; the 9th (A) just makes it shimmer. That is the whole sound of neo-soul: the same functional progressions as gospel and jazz, voiced with 9ths and 13ths.

### Alterations

On dominant chords only, the 5th and 9th can be raised or lowered: **b9, #9, b5/#11, #5/b13**. Each one adds tension that resolves by half step into the next chord. In the piece: F#7#5 (bars 2, 10, 16, 22, 24, 32, 46), B7#9 (bar 25) and A7b9 (bars 6, 14, 28, 36, 54).

### Avoid notes

Some scale notes clash when sustained in a chord because they sit a half step above a chord tone:

- **Natural 11 on major and dominant chords.** G over Dmaj9 rubs against F#. Use #11 (G# over D) instead, or keep G short as a passing note.
- **b9 on major or minor chords.** Eb over Dmaj9 sounds like a mistake; b9 belongs on dominants.
- **The 11 on a dominant needs the 3rd left out.** That is why A11 is usually voiced as A9sus4.

### Voicing: which notes you actually play

A 13th chord has seven notes and a guitar has six strings, so you choose. Leave out notes in this order: the 5th first, then the root (if a bass player or the bass note covers it), and never the 3rd or 7th unless you want an open, ambiguous sound. The piece's voicings show this:

### Upper structures: the secret of smooth extended chords

The top four notes of an extended chord are often a simpler chord. **A maj9 chord contains the m7 chord a 3rd above its root**: Gmaj9 (G B D F# A) holds Bm7 (B D F# A), and Dmaj9 holds F#m7 (F# A C# E). An **m9 chord contains the maj7 chord a minor 3rd above**: Em9 (E G B D F#) holds Gmaj7. That is why moving between Gmaj9, F#m7, Bm9 and Dmaj9 feels like barely moving at all: most of the notes are shared.

## Part 3 — The chords of D major and what they do

Build a 7th chord on each note of the D major scale, using only D major's notes, and you get seven chords. Seven of the piece's twelve chords come straight from this list. The other five are dominants borrowed to point at chords in it.

### The diatonic chords of D major

| Degree | Roman numeral | Triad | 7th chord | Version in the piece | Function |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | I | D | Dmaj7 | Dmaj9 | Tonic: home, at rest |
| 2 | ii | Em | Em7 | Em9, Em11 | Predominant: leads to V |
| 3 | iii | F#m | F#m7 | F#m7 | Tonic substitute: a soft step away from home |
| 4 | IV | G | Gmaj7 | Gmaj9 | Predominant: departure, lift |
| 5 | V | A | A7 | A13, A7b9 | Dominant: tension pulling to I |
| 6 | vi | Bm | Bm7 | Bm9 | Tonic substitute: home in minor |
| 7 | viiø | C#dim | C#m7b5 | C#m7b5 | Dominant in D; in this piece it acts as iiø of B minor |

The pattern of qualities is the same in every major key: **maj7, m7, m7, maj7, 7, m7, m7b5**. Learn it once and you can write the chords of any key from the table in Part 1.

### Harmonic function: home, away, tension

Every chord does one of three jobs:

- **Tonic (T)**: rest. I, plus its substitutes vi and iii, which share two or three notes with it (Dmaj9 holds the notes of F#m7; Bm9 contains every note of Dmaj7).
- **Predominant (PD)**: moving away, preparing tension. IV and ii.
- **Dominant (D)**: tension that wants to resolve. V, and viiø.

Most music cycles **T → PD → D → T**. In bars 4–7 that is Em11 (PD) → A13 (D) → Gmaj9 (PD again) → A13, A7b9 (D) → Dmaj9 (T).

### Why the dominant pulls

A dominant 7th contains a **tritone** between its 3rd and 7th. In A7 that is C# and G. The tritone is unstable, and each note resolves by the smallest step: **C# rises to D** (the leading tone to the tonic) and **G falls to F#** (the 7th falls to the 3rd of D). That two-note motion is the engine of tonal harmony, and Part 8 builds on it as guide tones.

### Secondary dominants: borrowing a V for any chord

Any major or minor chord in the key can be treated as a temporary tonic and given its own dominant 7th, built a 5th above it. This is a **secondary dominant**, written V/x. The piece uses three:

| Secondary dominant | Its target | Notes outside D major | In the piece |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F#7#5 = V/vi | Bm9 | A# (3rd, leads up to B) | Bars 2, 10, 24, 32, 46 lead to Bm9; bars 16 and 22 swerve to Gmaj9 |
| B7#9 = V/ii | Em9 | D# (3rd, leads up to E) | Bar 25 into bar 26 |
| E13 = V/V | A13 (by way of Em9) | G# (3rd, leads up to A) | Bar 12 (to Em9, then A13); bar 30 (to Gmaj9) |

The tell-tale sign of a secondary dominant is a raised note one half step below the target's root: A# before B, D# before E, G# before A.

### Minor ii–V: the turnaround into B minor

In minor keys, the ii chord is half-diminished. **C#m7b5 → F#7#5** is the minor ii–V of B minor (iiø → V/vi). It appears at the end of the Main flow (bars 16 and 22). Both times, instead of landing on Bm9, it lands on Gmaj9. That swerve, a deceptive resolution, is explained in Part 4.

### Borrowed colour: A7b9

The b9 of A is Bb, a note from D **minor**, not D major. Borrowing notes from the parallel minor is called **modal interchange** or **modal mixture**. A7b9 darkens the dominant just before Dmaj9, and the Bb falls by half step to A, the 5th of D, so the darkness resolves into light.

## Part 4 — Why the progression moves: a section-by-section reading

The Quietest Hour avoids the obvious V → I move most of the time. It lands on Dmaj9 only five times (bars 7, 15, 21, 29, 55), and each time after a full ii–V or IV–V approach. Everywhere else it circles home through IV, iii and vi. That is what keeps it floating.

### Section 1 — Opening (bars 1–8): the whole story in miniature

| Bar | Chords | Roman numerals | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Gmaj9 | IV | Starts away from home, on the lifted, open IV chord |
| 2 | F#m7 → F#7#5 | iii → V/vi | Root steps down a half step; then A rises to A#, turning iii into the dominant of B minor |
| 3 | Bm9 | vi | Resolution, but to the minor home, not D |
| 4 | Em11 → A13 | ii → V | Circle-of-fifths motion: B → E → A |
| 5 | Gmaj9 | IV | V falls back to IV instead of resolving: a soft, open-ended move |
| 6 | A13 → A7b9 | V → V(b9) | The dominant again, darker on beat 3 |
| 7 | Dmaj9 | I | First arrival home |
| 8 | (Dmaj9 rings) | I | Space |

Bars 1–3 are the most famous move in neo-soul and smooth soul: **IVmaj7 → iii7 → V/vi → vi**, often called the "4–3–6" or the "Just the Two of Us" progression. The root slides down by step (G → F#) and then jumps down a 5th (F# → B), so the ear hears both smooth motion and strong resolution.

### Section 2 — Main flow (bars 9–22): the same road, more side streets

- **Bars 9–11**: Gmaj9 → F#7#5 → Bm9. The 4–3–6 move, with the iii chord skipped and its dominant version used directly.
- **Bars 12–13**: E13 → Em9. E13 is V/V, but instead of resolving to A it turns minor on the same root. The G# falls a half step to G. This "major II to minor ii" shift is a gospel and neo-soul favourite: it delays the cadence and adds a sigh.
- **Bars 13–15**: Em9 → A13 → A7b9 → Dmaj9. A complete ii–V–I. This is the strongest cadence in tonal music.
- **Bar 16**: C#m7b5 → F#7#5. A minor ii–V aimed at B minor.
- **Bar 17**: Gmaj9, not Bm9. A **deceptive resolution**: the ear expects B minor and gets G major. It works because Gmaj9 contains B, D, F# and A, the whole Bm7 chord, so it sounds like B minor with a new root beneath it.
- **Bars 17–21**: Gmaj9 → F#m7 → Bm9 → Em9, A13 → Dmaj9. The 4–3–6 move again, then ii–V–I.
- **Bar 22**: C#m7b5 → F#7#5 again, and again it swerves to Gmaj9, this time to launch the peak.

### Section 3 — Emotional peak (bars 23–36): faster harmonic rhythm

The peak puts two chords in most bars, so the harmony moves twice as fast. That is a classic way to raise intensity without getting louder.

- **Bar 25**: Bm9 → B7#9. Same root, minor to dominant. The D natural of Bm9 becomes the #9 of B7#9, and the new D# drives up to E.
- **Bars 25–29**: B7#9 → Em9 → Em11 → A13, A7b9 → Dmaj9. Down the circle of fifths: B → E → A → D.
- **Bar 29**: Dmaj9 → F#m7. Tonic to its substitute iii, roots a 3rd apart; every note of F#m7 is already inside Dmaj9.
- **Bars 29–30**: D → F#m → Bm. Roots falling by 3rds then a 5th; every chord shares notes with the last.
- **Bar 30 → 31**: E13 → Gmaj9. V/V goes to IV, not V. G# falls a half step to G, and D stays put as the 5th of G. Another sidestep that keeps the peak from closing.
- **Bars 33–34**: Bm9, A13 → Gmaj9, F#m7 → Em9. Roots walk straight down the scale: **B A G F# E**. Diatonic stepwise descent, like a bass line walking downstairs.
- **Bar 36**: A13 → A7b9. A full dominant, setting up a big arrival.

### Section 4 — Pensive pullback (bars 37–48): the deceptive cadence

- **Bar 36 → 37**: A7b9 → Bm9. The ear expects Dmaj9 after the dominant; it gets B minor. This is a **deceptive cadence (V → vi)**, the textbook way to turn a climax into reflection. C# rises to D and G falls to F#, exactly as if resolving to D, but B arrives in the bass.
- **Bars 37–40**: Bm9 and Em11, each held for two bars. Harmonic rhythm slows to one chord per two bars: space and silence, written into the harmony.
- **Bars 41–44**: Gmaj9 → F#m7 → Em9 → A13. IV → iii → ii → V, roots stepping down then leaping up. A13 falls back to Gmaj9 (bar 45) instead of resolving.
- **Bars 45–48**: Gmaj9 → F#7#5 → Bm9, ringing. The 4–3–6 move one last time, ending on B minor and suspended.

### Section 5 — Final descent (bars 49–55): the long way home

| Bars | Chords | Roman numerals |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 49–52 | Gmaj9 → F#m7 → Em11 → A13 | IV → iii → ii → V |
| 53–55 | Gmaj9 → A7b9 → Dmaj9 | IV → V → I |

The first line descends by step (G F# E) and then rises to A. The second is the most basic cadence in music, **IV → V → I**, made dark by the b9 and then fully resolved. Ending on the only V → I of the section, after the ritardando, makes the final Dmaj9 feel earned.

### The big picture

- **One road, travelled five ways.** Almost every phrase starts on Gmaj9 and heads towards either Bm9 or Dmaj9.
- **Home is postponed.** V → I happens five times in 55 bars. V → IV and V → vi keep resolution soft or deferred.
- **Harmonic rhythm shapes emotion.** One chord per bar in calm sections, two in the peak, one per two bars in the pullback.

## Part 5 — Why these chords fit the flow

The chords fit because each one shares most of its notes with the next, and the notes that do change move by a half step or whole step. Four forces make that happen.

| A7b9 → Dmaj9 | A C# E G Bb | D F# A C# E | A, C#, E | G→F#, Bb→A |
| E13 → Em9 | E G# B D F# C# | E G B D F# | E, B, D, F# | G#→G, C# drops out |

The Bm9 → Em11 → A13 chain shares four or five notes at every step. That is why the descent feels like one long breath.

### 2. Smooth voice leading

When notes do move, they move as little as possible: mostly a half step, sometimes a whole step, almost never a leap. A half step is the strongest pull in music because it is the smallest. Look at the half steps the piece relies on:

- **A# → B** (F#7#5 → Bm9): leading tone into B minor.
- **Bb → A** (A7b9 → Dmaj9): flat 9 down to the 5th of D.
- **G → F#** (any A chord → Dmaj9): 7th of A down to 3rd of D.
- **G# → G** (E13 → Em9 or Gmaj9): the major-to-minor sigh.
- **D# → E** (B7#9 → Em9): leading tone into E minor.

Each altered note in the piece is there to set up one of these half steps.

### 3. Root motion

The distance between roots decides how strong a move feels:

| Root motion | Strength | Feeling | Examples in the piece |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Down a 5th / up a 4th | Strongest | Resolution, forward drive | B→E, E→A, A→D, F#→B, C#→F# |
| Down a step | Gentle, flowing | Walking, sighing | G→F# (bars 1–2), B→A→G→F#→E (bars 33–35) |
| Up a step | Surprise | Deceptive, fresh | A→B (A7b9 → Bm9, bars 36–37), F#→G (F#7#5 → Gmaj9, bars 16–17 and 22–23) |
| By a 3rd | Soft, many shared notes | Colour change, little tension | D→F# (Dmaj9 → F#m7, bar 29), E→G (E13 → Gmaj9, bars 30–31) |
| Same root, new quality | Colour shift | Brightening or darkening | Bm9→B7#9 (bar 25), E13→Em9 (bars 12–13), F#m7→F#7#5 (bar 2) |

The piece balances strong 5th motion (to drive) with stepwise and same-root moves (to float). Too much 5th motion sounds like a jazz exercise; too much stepwise motion sounds aimless.

### 4. A steady colour palette

Every chord carries a 7th, and most carry a 9th, 11th or 13th. Because the colour stays constant, no chord jumps out of the texture. Swap one chord for a plain triad and you would hear it immediately as thinner and brighter than its neighbours.

### 5. Tension and release, in measured doses

Rank the chords by tension and the piece reads like a heartbeat:

| Tension level | Chords | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1, at rest | Dmaj9 | Tonic |
| 2, soft | Bm9, F#m7, Gmaj9 | Tonic substitutes and the gentle IV |
| 3, moving | Em9, Em11, C#m7b5 | Predominants, preparing tension |
| 4, pulling | A13, E13 | Dominants with natural extensions |
| 5, strongest | F#7#5, B7#9, A7b9 | Altered dominants, with half-step pulls |

Tension rises through each phrase and drops at its end. The altered dominants sit just before resolutions, never at the start of a phrase.

### 6. The melody agrees with the chords

The written motifs use notes that are chord tones or colour tones of the chord underneath. The hook in bars 23 and 31 plays D, E, F#, A and B over Gmaj9: the 5th, 6th (13), 7th, 9th and 3rd of G. Nothing in it rubs against the chord.

## Part 6 — Chords that don't belong, and why

No chord is wrong in every song, but plenty are wrong in this one. A chord fails here when it has no job in D major, when its notes rub against the melody or the chords around it, or when it gives the ear a different home. Five tests catch almost every bad choice.

### The five tests

1. **Function.** Is it diatonic to D major, a dominant (or substitute dominant) of a diatonic chord, or borrowed from D minor? If none, it has no reason to be there.
2. **Clash.** Does it contain a note a half step from a note that is sustained around it, such as F natural against the melody's F#? Half-step rubs between held notes sound like mistakes.
3. **Voice leading.** Can its notes move to the next chord by step? If every voice has to leap, the flow breaks.
4. **Home.** Does it make another chord sound like the tonic? Too many notes outside D major, or a strong dominant aimed away from D's family, will move the key.
5. **Colour.** Does it match the 7th-and-9th palette? A bare triad or a power chord sticks out.

### Chords that would fight this arrangement

| Chord | Notes | Notes outside D major | Why it fails here | When it could work |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| G#m7 | G# B D# F# | G#, D# | Root a tritone from D, so it undermines home. No path into the next chord: every voice leaps or rubs. | Rarely. The half-diminished G#m7b5 (G# B D F#) is the usable version: one outside note, smooth into A13 |
| C#maj7 | C# E# G# B# | E#, G#, B# | Three notes outside the key, and a major chord on the leading tone has no function. | Almost never in D major |
| F#maj7 | F# A# C# E# | A#, E# | A# and E# clash with the A and E held in Gmaj9, Bm9 and the hook. It turns F# into a home of its own. | Only after a key change to F# or B |

| Am7 in place of A13 | A C E G | C | The C natural cancels C#, the leading tone. The dominant loses its pull, and D starts to sound like V of G. | As the first half of a ii–V into Gmaj9 (Am7 → D7 → Gmaj9) |

### Right chord, wrong place

Position matters as much as the chord itself:

- **Dmaj9 in bar 1** would start at home and remove the sense of departure that Gmaj9 gives.
- **Dmaj9 instead of Bm9 in bar 37** would close the peak with a full stop. The deceptive cadence into Bm9 is what turns the climax into reflection.
- **An altered dominant at the start of a phrase** (bar 9 or 41) would put the tension in the wrong place. Tension belongs at the end of a phrase.
- **Two chords per bar in Section 4** would erase the space the section exists for.

### Avoid notes inside the piece's own chords

## Part 7 — How to build a strong progression

There is no single perfect progression, but a strong one has a clear home, a reason for every move, smooth voice leading and tension placed at phrase ends. The method below builds one in seven steps; the Quietest Hour follows it exactly.

### The seven-step method

1. **Choose the key and the mood.** Major for warmth (D major), its relative minor for melancholy (B minor). Neo-soul often sits between the two.
2. **Set the phrase length.** Four or eight bars per phrase. Decide where the phrase ends; that is where the cadence goes.
3. **Write the functional skeleton.** Fill the bars with T, PD and D in that order, ending on T (a full cadence) or on D (a half cadence that keeps going). Example: T – PD – D – T.
4. **Choose chords for each function.** T = I, vi or iii. PD = IV or ii. D = V or viiø. Start with IV instead of I to begin "away" (as bar 1 does).
5. **Add secondary dominants** in front of any chord you want to emphasise: V/vi before vi, V/ii before ii, V/V before V. Give them tension (b9, #9, #5) only where you want extra pull.
6. **Colour every chord** to the same level: maj9 for I and IV, m9 or m11 for ii, iii and vi, 13 or altered for dominants.
7. **Check the voice leading.** Play the chords and follow the top note and the 3rds and 7ths (Part 8). Change voicings until each note moves by step or stays.

### Progression formulas worth knowing

| Name | Roman numerals | In D major | Feel | In the piece |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |

| 4–3–6 ("Just the Two of Us") | IVmaj7 – iii7 (or III7) – vi7 | Gmaj9 – F#m7 / F#7#5 – Bm9 | Neo-soul signature, bittersweet | Bars 1–3, 9–11, 17–19, 23–25, 31–33, 45–47 |

### Cadences: how phrases end

| Cadence | Motion | In D | Effect | In the piece |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Authentic | V → I | A13 → Dmaj9 | Full stop | Bars 6–7, 14–15, 20–21, 28–29, 54–55 |
| Half | anything → V | Em11 → A13 | Comma, question | Bars 4, 44, 52 |

| Plagal | IV → I | Gmaj9 → Dmaj9 | Gentle, "amen" | Not used |

### Rules of thumb

- **End phrases on a cadence.** Every 4 or 8 bars, give the ear a comma (half cadence) or a full stop (authentic).
- **Save the strongest cadence for the most important moment.** The Quietest Hour saves its plainest IV–V–I for the last three bars.
- **Balance strong and soft root motion.** Mix falling 5ths with steps and 3rds.
- **Speed up the harmony to build, slow it down to release.** Two chords per bar at the peak, one per two bars in the pullback.
- **Let the melody veto chords.** If a long melody note is an avoid note over a chord, change the chord or the note.
- **Repeat, then vary.** Reuse a strong progression (4–3–6 appears six times), changing one chord each time.

## Part 8 — Guide tones: the thread through the changes

The 3rd and 7th of each chord are its **guide tones**. They define the chord's quality (major or minor, major 7th or dominant 7th), and from chord to chord they usually move by a half step or not at all. Play only the guide tones and you can still hear the whole progression.

### Why the 3rd and 7th

- The **root** is often played by the bass. The **5th** is the same in major, minor and dominant chords, so it says little.
- The **3rd** says major or minor. The **7th** says maj7, dominant 7 or m7.
- When roots fall by a 5th, **the 7th of one chord falls a step to the 3rd of the next**, and **the 3rd of one chord stays to become the 7th of the next**. That is the voice-leading engine behind every ii–V–I.

### How to use guide tones

- **Comping.** On guitar, play just root, 3rd and 7th (a **shell voicing**) on the low three or four strings, for example 3-x-4-4 for Gmaj7 (G F# B) and 5-x-5-6 for A7 (A G C#). Shells leave room for the melody and sound clean at any volume.
- **Melody and improvisation.** Land on a guide tone on beat 1 of each new chord, especially a 7th falling to the next 3rd. The listener hears the changes even with no chords playing. This is called "playing the changes".
- **Arranging.** Give the guide-tone lines to an inner voice, a pad or a second guitar. The texture stays connected even when the top melody leaps.
- **Bass and reharmonization.** Before changing a chord, check whether the guide-tone lines still move by step. If they do, the new chord will usually sound right.

## Part 9 — Basslines for the sheet music

A bassline does three jobs: it states the root so the chord is clear, it drives the rhythm, and it connects one chord to the next. In The Quietest Hour the guitar plays its own bass notes, so a bass part must support without crowding: roots on the downbeat, approach notes into each change, and plenty of space.

### Principles

1. **Root on beat 1 of every chord change.** It anchors the extended voicing above. Gmaj9 as voiced on the sheet has no 3rd, so the bass G is what tells the ear "G major".
2. **Chord tones on strong beats.** Root, 5th and octave on beats 1 and 3. Use the 7th or 3rd on weaker beats for colour.
3. **Approach notes on the last beat before a change.** Lead into the next root from a half step below or above (chromatic approach), from the scale step next to it (diatonic approach), or from its 5th (dominant approach).
4. **Follow the chord's real 5th.** F#7#5 has D, not C#, as its 5th; C#m7b5 has G, not G#. A natural 5th under an altered chord clashes.
5. **Stay low and out of the guitar's way.** Keep the bass between low E (E1) and about the 7th fret of the G string (D3). The guitar's bass notes sit an octave higher.
6. **Match the section.** Long notes and silence where the guitar floats, moving 8ths and 16ths where it digs in.

### Neo-soul bass vocabulary

- **Swung 16ths.** Play the second and fourth 16ths of each beat slightly late, matching the guitar's swing.
- **Ghost notes.** Muted, barely-there octave or root notes on the "a" of a beat (the last 16th). They add groove without adding pitch.
- **Slides into the root.** Slide up from two frets below on beat 1, especially into Bm9 and Dmaj9.
- **The octave pop.** Root on beat 1, octave on the "and" of 2 or beat 4.
- **Pedal point.** Hold one bass note while chords change above. In the pullback, a B pedal under Bm9 and Em11 would deepen the suspended feeling.
- **Leave the downbeat.** In the most rubato moments, enter on beat 2 or let the guitar's own bass note speak alone.

### Bassline by section

- **Section 1 (bars 1–8, pp):** whole and half notes, one 5th on beat 4 as a pickup. Nothing busy under the rubato.
- **Section 2 (bars 9–22, mp):** root (dotted quarter), 5th on the "and" of 2, octave on 3, chromatic approach on the "and" of 4. A slow sway.
- **Section 3 (bars 23–36, mf):** root, ghosted octave, 7th, 5th, octave and a 16th-note approach. Busier, matching the double-stops.
- **Section 4 (bars 37–48, p):** one whole note per chord, left to decay. Silent in the let-ring bars.
- **Section 5 (bars 49–55, pp):** root and 5th with a quarter-note approach, slowing with the ritardando. A single low D under the final arpeggio.

## Part 10 — Theory you also need

Chords and progressions are half the picture. To write, improvise over and arrange music like The Quietest Hour you also need scales for each chord, an understanding of melody notes that are not in the chord, rhythm, form, reharmonization, and the guitar fretboard.

### The modes of D major

Play the D major scale starting from each of its notes and you get seven modes. Each mode fits the diatonic chord built on the same note.

| Mode | Notes | Fits | Characteristic note |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| D Ionian | D E F# G A B C# | Dmaj9 | C# (major 7th) |
| E Dorian | E F# G A B C# D | Em9, Em11 | C# (natural 6th in a minor key) |
| F# Phrygian | F# G A B C# D E | F#m7 | G (flat 2nd) |
| G Lydian | G A B C# D E F# | Gmaj9 | C# (#4, the floating sound of IV) |
| A Mixolydian | A B C# D E F# G | A13 | G (flat 7th) |
| B Aeolian | B C# D E F# G A | Bm9 | G (flat 6th) |
| C# Locrian | C# D E F# G A B | C#m7b5 | D (flat 2nd) and G (flat 5th) |

### Chord-scales for the non-diatonic chords

| Chord | Scale | Notes | Why |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| F#7#5 | F# altered (7th mode of G melodic minor) | F# G A A# C D E | Contains every tension: b9 (G), #9 (A), #11 (C), #5 (D) |
| F#7#5, softer | F# Phrygian dominant (5th mode of B harmonic minor) | F# G A# B C# D E | Stays inside B minor, so it points clearly at Bm9 |
| B7#9 | B altered, or E harmonic minor from B | B C D D# F G A / B C D# E F# G A | Both contain D (#9) and D# (3rd) |
| E13 | E Mixolydian or E Lydian dominant | E F# G# A B C# D / E F# G# A# B C# D | Lydian dominant removes the A, an avoid note against G# |
| A7b9 | A half-whole diminished | A Bb C C# D# E F# G | Contains b9, #9, #11 and 13, all at once |

### Pentatonic shortcuts

The **B minor pentatonic** (B D E F# A) has the same notes as D major pentatonic. It sounds good over Dmaj9, Bm9, Em9, Em11 and Gmaj9 (where it gives 3rd, 5th, 13th, 7th and 9th). Over A13 keep its D short. Over the altered dominants, switch to a scale from the table above, or simply target the chord's 3rd.

### Non-chord tones: melody notes outside the chord

A melody note that is not in the chord is fine if it moves correctly. The common types:

| Type | How it moves | Example in the piece |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Passing tone | Steps between two chord tones | E between F# and D in the fill motif over Bm9 (bar 19) |
| Neighbour tone | Steps away and back | B–C#–B on the high e string (7h9p7) in bar 19 |
| Appoggiatura | Leaps to a non-chord tone on a strong beat, then steps to a chord tone | A peak bend that starts a whole step below a chord tone and rises into it |
| Suspension | Holds a note from the last chord, then resolves down | Hold G (the 7th of A13) over the new Dmaj9 and let it fall to F#: a classic 4–3 suspension |
| Anticipation | Arrives early, before its chord | Any melody note played on the "a" of beat 4, tied over the bar line |
| Slide (glissando) | Glides by step into a target | The hook's 10/12 and 9/11 slides in bars 23 and 31 |

### Voice-leading rules

1. Keep common tones in the same voice.
2. Move other voices by the smallest interval available.
3. Resolve tendency tones: leading tones up (A# → B, C# → D), 7ths down (G → F#), b9s down (Bb → A).
4. Avoid moving every voice in the same direction by the same interval (parallel motion) unless you want a deliberate "planing" effect.
5. On guitar, choose the voicing of the next chord that is closest on the fretboard; that usually gives the smoothest leading as well.

### Rhythm, swing and rubato

- **Metre.** 4/4: four beats per bar, strongest on 1, secondary on 3.
- **Subdivision.** The 16th note is the pulse unit: 1 e & a, 2 e & a.
- **Swung 16ths.** Each pair of 16ths is played long-short. A ratio around 60:40 (the score uses 62%) gives the lazy, behind-the-beat neo-soul feel; 67:33 is a full triplet swing.
- **Rubato.** Tempo bends with the phrase: slight push into a peak, slight pull at a phrase end. Keep the bar lines in your head even when the time stretches.
- **Syncopation.** Accents on off-beats (the "&" and "a") create forward motion; the peak's hook lands its slide on the "&" of beat 2.
- **Harmonic rhythm.** How often chords change. It is a tool of form: one per bar (calm), two per bar (build), one per two bars (release).

### Form and phrasing

The piece is **through-composed** (no verse–chorus repeats), but it reuses cells. The 4–3–6 move (Gmaj9 → F#m7 or F#7#5 → Bm9) returns six times, each in new surroundings. Phrases mostly run in 4-bar units, with 2-bar extensions (the turnarounds in bars 16 and 22, the let-ring bars) breaking the symmetry so the music never feels mechanical. Dynamics (pp → mp → mf → p → pp) and register (low in the opening, high double-stops at the peak, low and sparse at the end) are part of the form too.

### Reharmonization toolkit

Use these to make your own version of the progression. Each keeps the guide-tone lines moving by step.

- **Tritone substitution:** replace a dominant with the dominant a tritone away (A13 → Eb9).
- **Secondary dominants:** add V/x before any chord (D7 before Gmaj9, C#7 before F#m7).
- **Related ii:** put the ii chord of a dominant before it (Bm7b5 → E7 or Bm9 → E13, as bar 30 does).
- **Modal interchange:** borrow chords from D minor (Gm6, Bbmaj7, C9).
- **Passing diminished chords:** A#dim7 between A13 and Bm9.
- **Pedal point:** keep one bass note under changing chords.
- **Line cliché:** one inner voice moves chromatically under a held chord (Bm → Bm(maj7) → Bm7 → Bm6).
- **Planing:** slide one voicing shape up or down the fretboard by step, ignoring the key, for a moment of colour.
- **Sus chords:** replace a dominant with its sus version (A13sus or Em7/A) for a softer pull.

### Enharmonic spelling

Spell each note by its job, not its sound. In F#7#5 the 3rd is **A#** (it is the 3rd above F#, a kind of A). In A7b9 the flat 9th is **Bb** (a kind of B). The two notes sound the same but resolve differently: A# rises to B, Bb falls to A. Correct spelling makes the resolution visible on the page.

### The guitar fretboard

- **Positions.** Most of the piece's voicings sit between frets 2 and 7. Learn each chord in at least two positions so you can choose the closest one.
- **Shell voicings.** Root, 3rd and 7th on strings 6-4-3 or 5-4-3. Add one colour note on string 2 for a full neo-soul voicing.
- **Drop-2 voicings.** Take a close-position 7th chord and drop the second-highest note an octave. The result fits under four fingers on adjacent strings.
- **CAGED.** The five open-chord shapes (C, A, G, E, D) move up the neck and cover every position. Use them to find where any chord tone lives.
- **Natural harmonics.** Frets 12, 7 and 5 give the root's octave, 5th and double octave of each open string. They are free pad notes in D major: E, A, D, G, B and F# all fit.

### Ear training

1. Sing the bass root of each chord while playing the progression.
2. Sing the upper guide-tone line from Part 8 while playing chords.
3. Learn to hear chord quality: major 7th (dreamy), minor 7th (soft), dominant (pulling), half-diminished (dark, unresolved).
4. Listen to neo-soul and gospel recordings and write down the root motion only. You will hear the 4–3–6, ii–V and deceptive moves from this book.

## Glossary and practice plan

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| Altered dominant | A dominant 7th chord with a raised or lowered 5th or 9th (F#7#5, B7#9, A7b9) |
| Approach note | A bass or melody note a step above or below the next target, played just before it |
| Avoid note | A scale note that clashes when held over a chord, usually a half step above a chord tone |
| Cadence | The chord motion that ends a phrase: authentic, half, deceptive, plagal |
| Chord-scale | The scale that fits a given chord, used for melody and improvisation |
| Common tone | A note shared by two consecutive chords |
| Deceptive cadence | A dominant that resolves somewhere other than I, usually vi |
| Diatonic | Belonging to the key (all notes from the key's scale) |
| Dominant | The V chord; also any major-minor 7th chord that pulls a 5th down |
| Enharmonic | Two names for one pitch (A# and Bb) |
| Extension | 9th, 11th or 13th added above the 7th |
| Function | A chord's job: tonic, predominant or dominant |
| Guide tones | The 3rd and 7th of a chord |
| Harmonic rhythm | How often the chords change |
| Leading tone | A note a half step below a target that pulls up into it (C# to D, A# to B) |
| Modal interchange | Borrowing chords or notes from the parallel key (D minor in D major) |
| Relative minor | The minor key sharing a key signature with a major key (B minor for D major) |

| Tritone | Six half steps; the unstable interval inside every dominant 7th |
